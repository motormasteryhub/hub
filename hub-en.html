📖 How to Use This Guide

Before you begin, take two minutes to read these guidelines — they make all the difference in your child's results! 🌟

📅

Ideal frequency

Practice 3 to 5 times a week. Consistency beats quantity — a few minutes most days works far better than one long hour on the weekend.

⏱️

Session length

Each activity takes 5 to 10 minutes. Don't push past the suggested time — enjoyment is part of the learning! Feel free to combine two activities in one session.

📈

Gradual progression

Always start with the Beginner activities before moving to Intermediate and Advanced. Each stage prepares your child's brain for the next challenge.

🎯

Pick the right age range

Find your child's age group in the index. If something feels too hard, simply drop down one range — that's completely normal and never a sign of being behind!

🏆

Celebrate every win

After each activity, praise genuinely. Try "You did amazing today!" or "I'm so proud of you!" Emotional encouragement genuinely speeds up learning.

😌

Keep it light

Present every activity as play, never homework. A relaxed, pressure-free atmosphere helps the brain learn faster and with much less resistance.

💬

A golden tip: pick one special moment of the day — right after snack time, or just before bath — and turn it into a little ritual between you and your child. That predictability builds a sense of safety and keeps kids coming back excited!

✋ Fine Motor Skills 15 activities

1Crumple Paper Balls

Beginner

You'll need: Used sheets of paper (notebook paper, newspaper, napkins)

  1. Give your child a sheet of paper.
  2. Ask them to crumple it with both hands into a tiny ball.
  3. Try making the balls smaller and smaller.
  4. Play 'who can make the roundest little ball?'

Why it works: Strengthens hand and finger muscles, preparing for a firmer pencil grip.

2Tear Paper Strips

Beginner

You'll need: Old magazine pages, newspaper or crepe paper

  1. Hold the sheet firmly with both hands.
  2. Pull with the fingers to tear it into strips.
  3. Try tearing in straight lines, then diagonally.
  4. Turn the torn strips into a picture or collage.

Why it works: Develops finger movement control and bimanual coordination (using both hands together).

3Match the Lids

Beginner

You'll need: Yogurt cups or small bottles with lids

  1. Remove the lids from several containers.
  2. Ask your child to match each lid to the right container.
  3. Encourage twisting the lid until it closes.
  4. Vary the container sizes to raise the challenge.

Why it works: Trains the pincer grasp and wrist rotation — essential movements for writing and drawing.

4Stack Sugar Cubes or Mini Blocks

Beginner

You'll need: Sugar cubes, empty matchboxes or building blocks

  1. Place the cubes on a flat surface.
  2. Ask your child to stack them one by one, carefully.
  3. Count how many they can stack before it falls.
  4. Each round, try to beat their own record.

Why it works: Exercises movement precision and concentration, training the child to act gently and with control.

5Draw Lines in the Air

Beginner

You'll need: No materials needed

  1. Stand facing your child.
  2. Draw a straight line in the air with your index finger.
  3. Ask them to copy your movement.
  4. Then try wavy lines, zigzags and circles.

Why it works: Prepares the arm and hand muscles for the movements of writing and drawing.

6Squeeze Play Dough

Intermediate

You'll need: Homemade play dough (flour, salt and water) or store-bought

  1. Give your child a piece of dough.
  2. Ask them to squeeze, pull and roll it.
  3. Try making a 'snake' (a long roll).
  4. Then flatten it with the palm to make a 'pizza'.

Why it works: Strengthens all the muscles of the hands and fingers, building stamina for fine-effort tasks.

7Thread Pasta onto Skewers

Intermediate

You'll need: Dry pasta (penne or rigatoni) and wooden skewers or thick sticks

  1. Stand a skewer upright in a lump of dough or clay.
  2. Show how to slide a piece of pasta down the skewer.
  3. Ask your child to keep threading, piece by piece.
  4. Count how many pieces fit before it's full.

Why it works: Sharpens hand-eye coordination and the pincer grasp — fundamental skills for writing.

8Lace Through the Holes

Intermediate

You'll need: Thick cardboard with punched holes, string or a shoelace

  1. Punch a row of holes in a piece of cardboard.
  2. Tie the string to one of the holes.
  3. Show how to lace the string from hole to hole.
  4. Let your child complete the path on their own.

Why it works: Develops fine coordination, perseverance and attention to detail.

9Bottle-Cap Spoon Relay

Intermediate

You'll need: Plastic spoons, bottle caps and wooden sticks

  1. Ask your child to balance a bottle cap on a spoon.
  2. Try passing the cap from spoon to spoon without dropping it.
  3. Increase the distance between the spoons.
  4. Time how many caps they can transfer in 5 minutes.

Why it works: Trains fine movement balance and the visual concentration needed for everyday tasks.

10Color Inside the Lines

Intermediate

You'll need: A sheet with large shapes drawn on it (circle, square, triangle) and crayons

  1. Show a large shape drawn on the page.
  2. Explain that the color has to stay 'inside the fence'.
  3. Encourage coloring the edges slowly first.
  4. Fill in the middle afterwards. Praise the careful work!

Why it works: Exercises hand movement control and trains the eye to guide the gesture with precision.

11Simple Jigsaw Puzzle

Advanced

You'll need: A 6-to-12-piece puzzle with large pieces

  1. Spread the pieces picture-side up.
  2. Find the edge pieces and start with them.
  3. Fit pieces by turning them and testing spots.
  4. Don't help unless your child asks — let them try!

Why it works: Develops spatial reasoning, perseverance and refined fine coordination while fitting pieces.

12Simple Paper Folding

Advanced

You'll need: Plain or colored sheets of paper

  1. Show how to fold the paper in half, lining up the corners.
  2. Ask your child to do the same on their own.
  3. Run a finger along the fold to crease it.
  4. Try making a simple little boat with one more fold.

Why it works: Trains motor precision, spatial planning and patience — skills that show up directly in writing.

13Big Buttons: Open & Close

Advanced

You'll need: An old piece of clothing with large buttons, or a button-practice toy

  1. Show how to open a button by passing it through the hole.
  2. Let your child try alone — the first ones will take several attempts.
  3. After opening them all, ask them to button up again.
  4. Praise every closed button: 'What skill!'

Why it works: Develops fine finger coordination and independence, preparing your child to dress on their own.

14Trace Simple Paths

Advanced

You'll need: Paper with simple mazes (or ones you draw) and a pencil

  1. Show the maze: where it starts and where it ends.
  2. Ask them to trace the path slowly with the pencil.
  3. Encourage staying on the line.
  4. Cheer when they reach the end!

Why it works: Integrates hand-eye coordination, planning and motor control — the essential base for pre-writing.

15Sort the Beans

Advanced

You'll need: A mix of beans, corn and chickpeas on a plate; three small bowls

  1. Show the three types of grain mixed together.
  2. Explain that each bowl gets a different type.
  3. Your child picks them up one by one with their fingers and places each in the right bowl.
  4. Do it together the first few times to show the logic.

Why it works: Exercises the pincer grasp, selective attention and categorizing — precursors of math and reading.

🏃 Gross Motor Skills 15 activities

1Floor Rolling

Beginner

You'll need: A safe spot on the floor (rug or grass)

  1. Lie on your side on the floor next to your child.
  2. Show how to roll slowly to the other side.
  3. Ask them to copy you.
  4. Have a 'rolling race' in a straight line.

Why it works: Develops body awareness, balance and spatial awareness in a playful, safe way.

2Walk the Line

Beginner

You'll need: Colored tape on the floor (or chalk outside)

  1. Stick a straight line of tape on the floor.
  2. Show how to walk stepping only on the line.
  3. First with arms stretched out to the sides.
  4. Then try it with hands on hips.

Why it works: Trains balance, concentration and body awareness — fundamental for all motor activities.

3Jump Over a Low Obstacle

Beginner

You'll need: A line of tape on the floor or a thin cushion

  1. Place the tape on the floor (or the cushion).
  2. Show how to jump with both feet at the same time.
  3. Let your child jump back and forth over it.
  4. Praise them and gradually raise the obstacle.

Why it works: Builds leg strength, dynamic balance and the courage to take on physical challenges.

4Animal Imitations

Beginner

You'll need: Open space to move around

  1. Announce: 'Let's be animals!'
  2. You call the animal and both of you act it out together:
  3. Duck (squatting, feet turned out), frog (jumping), elephant (arm as a trunk).
  4. Try at least 5 different animals each session.

Why it works: Develops body awareness, imitation — the basis of social learning — and overall muscle strength.

5Toss the Ball Up and Catch

Beginner

You'll need: A light plastic ball (soft type)

  1. Sit on the floor or stand up.
  2. Toss the ball up, not too high.
  3. Try to catch it with both hands.
  4. Raise the height little by little as your child succeeds.

Why it works: Trains reaction time, hand-eye coordination and movement anticipation.

6Dance and Freeze (Statue)

Intermediate

You'll need: Upbeat music (your phone works)

  1. Put on a happy song and dance together.
  2. When the music stops, both freeze completely.
  3. Whoever moves loses and starts again.
  4. Play for 5 minutes, alternating the pauses.

Why it works: Develops body self-control, musical rhythm and the ability to inhibit the urge to move.

7Roll the Ball Through the Goal

Intermediate

You'll need: A medium ball, small PET bottles or improvised cones

  1. Set up a 'gate' with two objects spaced apart.
  2. Show how to push the ball along the floor with the hands.
  3. Try to send the ball through the gate without knocking anything over.
  4. Increase the distance progressively.

Why it works: Sharpens controlled strength, aim and movement planning — precursors of team sports.

8Walk Backwards

Intermediate

You'll need: A hallway or safe space free of obstacles

  1. Explain you'll walk backwards, nice and slow.
  2. Keep arms open to feel the space.
  3. Mark a 6-foot distance and try to reach it.
  4. Then try stretching it to 12 feet.

Why it works: Builds the mental map of the body in space, proprioception and attention to the surroundings.

9Sock Ball into the Basket

Intermediate

You'll need: Rolled-up socks and a bucket or basket

  1. Place the basket about 3 feet away.
  2. Your child tries to land the sock ball inside.
  3. Give 5 tries and count the hits.
  4. Move the basket a foot farther each successful round.

Why it works: Trains aim, measured force and healthy frustration — learning that missing is part of the process.

10Up and Down the Stairs, Alternating Feet

Intermediate

You'll need: Your home stairs or a single step (a low platform works)

  1. Stay beside your child for safety.
  2. Teach alternating feet: left, right, left…
  3. Go up slowly first, then come down with the same rhythm.
  4. Count the steps out loud together.

Why it works: Develops laterality, dynamic balance and rhythm — foundations for coordination in sports.

11Cushion Walk

Advanced

You'll need: 5 to 8 cushions scattered on the floor

  1. Spread the cushions like 'stepping stones' across a river.
  2. Your child must hop cushion to cushion without touching the floor.
  3. If they touch, they start over.
  4. Gradually widen the spacing between cushions.

Why it works: Exercises dynamic balance, motor planning and quick decision-making.

12Simple Obstacle Course

Advanced

You'll need: Cushions, boxes, chairs and tape

  1. Build a mini course: crawl under the table, jump over a cushion, go around the box.
  2. Explain the route before starting.
  3. Your child completes the full course.
  4. Repeat 3 times and try to get faster each run.

Why it works: Develops motor sequencing, movement memory and spatial organization.

13One-Foot Balance

Advanced

You'll need: A safe space near a wall

  1. Stand next to your child.
  2. Ask them to lift one foot and hold still.
  3. Count out loud how many seconds they last.
  4. Switch feet and try to beat the record.

Why it works: Trains static balance and concentration — skills that carry over to posture and classroom attention.

14Combo Dance Moves

Advanced

You'll need: Upbeat music

  1. Create a simple sequence: clap, clap, spin, jump.
  2. Do it together slowly first.
  3. Speed up the music's rhythm.
  4. Add a new move every round.

Why it works: Integrates rhythm, sequential memory and whole-body coordination — essential skills for school learning.

15Roll the Ball with Your Foot

Advanced

You'll need: A soccer ball or medium plastic ball

  1. Place the ball on the floor about 20 inches from your child.
  2. Ask them to push the ball with their foot to a marked point.
  3. Try to keep the ball in a straight line.
  4. Repeat with the other foot.

Why it works: Develops foot-object coordination, balance in motion and body laterality.

🧠 Focus, Attention & Organization 20 activities

1Look and Point: What Changed?

Beginner

You'll need: 5 small objects on a table

  1. Place 5 objects on the table.
  2. Ask your child to look and memorize them.
  3. Ask them to close their eyes.
  4. Remove one object and ask: 'What's missing?'

Why it works: Trains visual memory and attention to detail — the base for reading and pattern recognition.

2Follow the Sound, Eyes Closed

Beginner

You'll need: A rattle, a spoon and pot, or anything that makes noise

  1. Your child closes their eyes.
  2. Make a noise in one corner of the room.
  3. Ask them to point to where the sound came from.
  4. Change the object and the spot; repeat 5 times.

Why it works: Develops auditory attention and spatial localization — fundamental for following instructions at school.

3Sort by Color

Beginner

You'll need: Colored boxes (or bowls) and mixed colorful objects (buttons, caps, play dough)

  1. Mix objects of 3 different colors.
  2. Show a box for each color.
  3. Ask your child to put each object in the right box.
  4. Praise: 'You found them all! What attention!'

Why it works: Exercises classification, selective attention and the beginnings of organized, categorical thinking.

4Count Objects with Your Finger

Beginner

You'll need: Any set of small objects (fruit, toys, bottle caps)

  1. Line up 1 to 5 objects in a row.
  2. Show how to point at each one and say the number.
  3. Ask them to do the same.
  4. Shuffle and count again.

Why it works: Develops number sense, sequential attention and control of the urge to skip steps.

5Listen and Repeat

Beginner

You'll need: No materials

  1. Say a simple word sequence (dog, sun, ball).
  2. Ask your child to repeat in the same order.
  3. Start with 2 words, then move to 3.
  4. Stretch as far as they can before mixing it up.

Why it works: Trains short-term auditory memory — essential for following instructions and learning new words.

6Shape Sorter Board

Beginner

You'll need: A shape-sorter toy or shapes cut from cardboard

  1. Mix the shapes outside the board.
  2. Ask your child to fit each shape in the right place.
  3. If they're stuck, help them rotate the piece until it fits.
  4. Timing it (no pressure) can make it more fun.

Why it works: Develops shape recognition, spatial reasoning and persistence in the face of error.

7The Quiet Game

Intermediate

You'll need: No materials

  1. Say: 'Let's see who can stay quiet the longest!'
  2. Both of you go completely silent.
  3. Whoever makes a sound first 'loses'.
  4. Try to stretch the time each round.

Why it works: Develops self-control, inhibition of the urge to talk and tolerance for stillness — essential for the classroom.

8Color from Biggest to Smallest

Intermediate

You'll need: A sheet with shapes of varied sizes and crayons

  1. Show shapes of different sizes on the page.
  2. Ask them to color the biggest first, then the middle one, then the smallest.
  3. Help them order visually before starting.
  4. Then try from smallest to biggest.

Why it works: Trains the notion of sequence and order — the base for math and for organizing ideas.

9Find the Hidden Pair

Intermediate

You'll need: 8 cards with 4 pairs of pictures (animals, fruits, shapes)

  1. Spread the cards face down.
  2. Your child flips two at a time trying to find the pair.
  3. If they miss, flip them back and try again.
  4. The game ends when all pairs are found.

Why it works: Exercises visual memory, concentrated attention and strategy — core cognitive skills for learning.

10Build a Picture Sequence

Intermediate

You'll need: Small cards with simple images (sun, cloud, flower, star)

  1. Create a sequence of 3 pictures on the table.
  2. Ask your child to continue the sequence with the remaining cards.
  3. Change the pattern and repeat.
  4. Let your child create a sequence for YOU to continue.

Why it works: Develops logical reasoning, pattern recognition and sustained attention.

11Put Toys Away by Category

Intermediate

You'll need: Scattered toys and 3 boxes or bags

  1. Spread several toys on the floor.
  2. Define: 'This box is for animals, this one for balls, this one for blocks.'
  3. Ask your child to put each toy in the right box.
  4. Praise: 'What organization! It's so much easier to find things later!'

Why it works: Trains categorizing, planning and the organization habits that cut down on daily mess.

12Follow a Picture Recipe

Intermediate

You'll need: A simple illustrated recipe (e.g. banana smoothie — banana + milk + cup)

  1. Show the recipe in pictures.
  2. Ask: 'What's the first ingredient? And the second?'
  3. Your child 'prepares' it by pointing at each step in order.
  4. If you can, make it for real together!

Why it works: Develops sequential thinking, reading visual instructions and planning tasks in steps.

13Copy a Simple Drawing

Advanced

You'll need: A drawn model, a blank sheet and a pencil

  1. Place a simple drawing (house, sun, tree) next to the blank sheet.
  2. Ask your child to copy it while looking at the model.
  3. Don't correct during the process — let them try.
  4. At the end, compare the two versions kindly.

Why it works: Exercises attention to detail, hand-eye coordination and staying focused on a task to the end.

14Build a Tower with Rules

Advanced

You'll need: Colored blocks or small boxes

  1. Say: 'Let's build a tower with rules!'
  2. Rule: yellow on the bottom, blue in the middle, red on top.
  3. Your child builds following the instruction.
  4. Increase to 4 or 5 levels with different colors.

Why it works: Trains instruction-following, working memory and sequential planning.

15Morning Routine Cards

Advanced

You'll need: Illustrated cards: wake up, wash face, brush teeth, get dressed, eat breakfast

  1. Shuffle the morning routine cards.
  2. Ask your child to put them in the correct order.
  3. Discuss: 'What happens if we get dressed before washing our face?'
  4. Tape the right sequence to their bedroom wall.

Why it works: Develops the sense of routine, causal thinking ('what comes before and after') and daily independence.

16Guess the Object by Touch

Advanced

You'll need: A cloth bag or opaque sack and 5 small objects

  1. Put the objects inside the bag.
  2. Your child reaches in without looking and grabs one.
  3. They describe what they feel: 'It's smooth, round, small…'
  4. They try to guess what it is before pulling it out.

Why it works: Stimulates tactile attention, descriptive vocabulary and focus on non-visual sensory information.

17Listen to a Short Story and Retell It

Advanced

You'll need: A simple children's book with few pages

  1. Read a 5-to-8-page story with expression.
  2. When you finish, close the book.
  3. Ask: 'What happened first? And at the end?'
  4. Let your child retell it in their own words.

Why it works: Develops narrative memory, sustained attention and the ability to organize events in sequence.

18Opposite Commands Game

Advanced

You'll need: Open space

  1. Explain the rule: when you say 'stand up', they sit. When you say 'sit down', they stand.
  2. Start slowly so they grasp the concept.
  3. Speed up the commands gradually.
  4. Laugh at the slip-ups — lightness is part of the learning!

Why it works: Trains inhibitory control and selective attention — central skills for behavioral self-regulation.

19Choose the Play Order

Advanced

You'll need: 3 different toys or activities

  1. Place 3 play options on the table.
  2. Ask your child to decide: 'Which first? Which second? Which third?'
  3. Respect the order they set.
  4. At the end ask: 'Did you like your order? Would you change anything?'

Why it works: Develops the ability to plan, make decisions and notice consequences — fundamental executive skills.

20Build the Story Scene

Advanced

You'll need: Dolls, plastic animals and building blocks

  1. Tell a mini-story: 'The puppy went to the park and saw a butterfly.'
  2. Ask your child to build the scene with the objects.
  3. After building, ask them to tell the story back.
  4. Add a new character to the story and watch them adapt the scene.

Why it works: Stimulates organized imagination, mental representation and attention to narrative detail.

🛡️ Safety note: at this age, always supervise activities with small parts (beans, pasta, caps, buttons) — little explorers love to taste things!

✋ Fine Motor Skills 15 activities

1Cutting with Safety Scissors

Beginner

You'll need: Blunt-tip safety scissors and colored paper

  1. Show how to hold the scissors with the thumb on top.
  2. Start by cutting paper freely.
  3. Then try cutting in a straight line.
  4. Make strips, then little squares — and turn them into a collage!

Why it works: Develops finger control, dominant-hand strength and the bimanual coordination essential for writing.

2Play-Dough Animals

Beginner

You'll need: Colored play dough

  1. Show how to make a little ball and a snake.
  2. Suggest creating an animal together: dog, turtle, fish.
  3. Make the body from the ball, the legs from little snakes.
  4. Let your child finish without corrections — the process is what matters!

Why it works: Exercises finger strength and flexibility, while sparking creativity and spatial planning.

3Spoon the Beans

Beginner

You'll need: Two bowls and dry beans (or other grains)

  1. Put all the beans in one bowl.
  2. With a teaspoon, transfer them one by one to the other bowl.
  3. Try not to drop any along the way.
  4. Time it and try to beat the record — no rushing.

Why it works: Trains fine motor control, visual attention and patience — essential traits for schoolwork.

4Paint with a Brush

Beginner

You'll need: Washable paint, a thick brush and a large sheet

  1. Dip the brush in paint and wipe off the excess.
  2. Paint freely on the big sheet.
  3. Try to fill the whole page.
  4. Mix two colors once each one is mastered on its own.

Why it works: Develops control of pressure and stroke direction — the base for handwriting and drawing.

5Zip and Unzip

Beginner

You'll need: A pencil case, bag or piece of clothing with a zipper

  1. Show how to hold the base of the zipper with one hand.
  2. With the other, pull the zipper up slowly.
  3. Then open it again.
  4. Repeat until it's smooth and independent.

Why it works: Exercises fine coordination and builds independence in self-care tasks.

6Sew on a Punched Card

Intermediate

You'll need: A punched card and colorful yarn

  1. Thread the yarn through the first hole.
  2. Pull it through and into the next, like a needle.
  3. Continue until the outline is complete.
  4. Make different shapes: star, heart, house.

Why it works: Develops fine coordination, perseverance and attention to sequencing — a direct base for cursive writing.

7Cut Out Simple Shapes

Intermediate

You'll need: Safety scissors and paper with shapes drawn on it (large circle, square)

  1. Show how to follow the line while cutting.
  2. Start with the circle (curves) — it's easier.
  3. Then cut the square, stopping at the corners.
  4. Build a little figure with the cut-out shapes.

Why it works: Sharpens scissor control, visual line-tracking and movement precision.

8Wrap the Rubber Band

Intermediate

You'll need: A pen or pencil and colorful rubber bands

  1. Place a rubber band at the tip of the pencil.
  2. With the fingers, wind it along the length.
  3. Wrap slowly all the way to the end.
  4. Unwind and repeat. Try a band of a different thickness.

Why it works: Strengthens the intrinsic hand muscles and exercises finger coordination with small objects.

9Trace the Dotted Lines

Intermediate

You'll need: A sheet with dotted figures and a pencil

  1. Show the figure formed by the dots.
  2. Ask them to connect the dots with lines.
  3. Start with simple shapes: triangle, square.
  4. Then try a dotted dog or house.

Why it works: Exercises pencil control and attention to detail, directly preparing for pre-writing.

10Fold the Napkin into Squares

Intermediate

You'll need: A paper napkin

  1. Fold the napkin in half.
  2. Fold it in half again.
  3. Unfold and look at the little squares formed.
  4. Try other folds: triangle, envelope.

Why it works: Trains folding precision, spatial orientation and bimanual coordination.

11Write Your Own Name

Advanced

You'll need: A handwriting notebook or sheet with the name in dotted letters, and a pencil

  1. Show the name in dotted letters.
  2. Ask your child to follow the traces with the pencil.
  3. Then write the name without dots for them to copy.
  4. Finally, try writing it from memory alone.

Why it works: Develops emergent writing, recognition of their own letters and identity — a base for literacy.

12Zigzag Cutting

Advanced

You'll need: Safety scissors and paper with a zigzag line drawn on it

  1. Show the zigzag line.
  2. Cut slowly following each diagonal.
  3. Encourage: 'Now turn to the other side!'
  4. Use the cut strips to make a decoration.

Why it works: Sharpens scissor control and hand-eye coordination on more complex cutting paths.

13Tie Simple Knots

Advanced

You'll need: A shoelace or fabric ribbon

  1. Show the simple knot step by step with your child's hands over yours.
  2. Pass the ribbon underneath, cross and pull through.
  3. Pull slowly to tighten.
  4. Repeat until they can do it alone.

Why it works: Exercises bimanual coordination and sequence memory, preparing for tying their own shoes.

14Draw Inside the Outline

Advanced

You'll need: A sheet with object outlines and colored pencils

  1. Pick a simple object: car, sun, flower.
  2. Ask them to color while respecting the edges.
  3. Then ask them to draw something inside the outline (e.g. windows on the car).
  4. Praise the care and the creativity.

Why it works: Integrates motor control, visual attention and creativity — skills that translate into writing and art.

15Pasta Necklace

Advanced

You'll need: Tube pasta (rigatoni) and string or yarn

  1. Cut a string the length of a necklace.
  2. Thread the end through the first piece of pasta.
  3. Keep stringing them on, alternating colors if you have them.
  4. Tie the ends and wear the necklace as a prize!

Why it works: Develops the fine pincer grasp, persistence and sequential vision — plus the satisfaction of a finished creation.

🏃 Gross Motor Skills 15 activities

1Two-Foot Square Jumps

Beginner

You'll need: Tape forming squares on the floor (or chalk outside)

  1. Make 4 squares in a straight row with tape.
  2. Show how to jump inside each one.
  3. Your child jumps square by square.
  4. Then try it with hands on hips.

Why it works: Exercises dynamic balance, leg strength and spatial awareness of their own body.

💡 Extra Tips for Parents

You're already doing your best for your child! Here are a few strategies to make everything even easier in daily life. 💛

01

If your child resists, don't force it

Resistance is normal, especially in the first weeks. Instead of pushing, try a different time of day, propose a challenge ("can you do it in 5 minutes?") or do the activity together. Your participation is the biggest motivator there is.

02

Use healthy competition

Kids love beating their own records. Phrases like "today you'll go even farther than yesterday!" work far better than competing against siblings or friends, which can breed frustration.

03

Cut distractions during activity time

TV off, phones in the drawer. These 10 minutes are sacred. A distraction-free environment trains the brain to focus — and that carries straight over to school and homework.

04

Don't correct constantly

Let your child try, miss and discover. Interrupting to fix every little thing breaks concentration and chips away at self-esteem. Guide only when needed — calmly and kindly.

05

Involve the whole family when you can

Dad, grandparents, older siblings — the more people join the games, the more fun and motivating it gets. And your child feels loved and supported by everyone around them.

06

Notice progress out loud

"Wow — last week you couldn't do this, and today you nailed it!" Observations like this show your child that effort pays off, and powerfully reinforce positive behavior.

07

Be patient with your own rhythm too

Some days you'll be exhausted, or your child simply won't want to. That's okay! A pause doesn't undo progress. What matters is coming back when you can — guilt-free.

08

Connect activities to everyday life

After practicing, point out the skill showing up in real life: "Look — the same focus you used in our game, you just used to pack your backpack!" That builds awareness and inner motivation.
